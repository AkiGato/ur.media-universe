Facilitator's kit · course three

Alternative Storytelling

Four weeks on narrative and game mechanics in education and wellbeing. Built on New Technologies in Storytelling and Gamification for Education and Mental Health and taught through the three projects developed inside it — an XR escape room, a flying-boat flow game, and an analogue journal game with an AI companion. The three tests the course runs on were found in that practice, not imported into it.

4 weeks · 16 sessions48 contact hoursStudio
01

The map

State the apparent contradiction in the first ten minutes. Students who have taken the first course of this programme know it names streaks, leaderboards and variable reward as weapons and forbids them outright. This course teaches them to build with the same vocabulary, and the thing that reconciles the two is a definition rather than a compromise.

They built worlds — storytelling, gamification, branded fictions — and invited the audience inside. Trust was not required, because the audience had entered the pact that cinema and literature rely on: disbelief suspended by consent. A fiction you have agreed to is not a lie. Media as Universe, §2.1

Suspension of disbelief is Coleridge's term, from 1817, and it appears in both source theses — which is the point worth making to the room. The mechanic is not the unit of judgement; the consent is. A streak serving a habit someone chose supports that person's own intention. The identical streak serving a subscription they are trying to cancel extracts from them. Same code, opposite direction of agency.

Three tests separate them, and the third one has a provenance the room should hear. It was not derived from theory. It was found by the author of the source thesis in her own project, in the middle of building something that worked:

The ethical question was, this flow was capturing, this boat could become a safe place one couldn't easily leave. On Cloudy Horizons

A designer noticing that her own good design had become difficult to leave, and treating that as a problem rather than a success metric, is the whole course in one sentence. Everything else is method for arriving at the same observation reliably.

Week one

The synergy and the pact

Storytelling and play as one subject. The measurable physiology of narration. The three-way distinction between a game, game-based learning and gamification. Disbelief suspended by consent, and what generative media did to it.

OutputA recall figure measured on the room, and a pact analysis of an existing work.
Week two

The permitted mechanics

Self-determination theory as the working criteria. Player archetypes, and why their origin in audience segmentation matters. Flow, and its four conditions. The three tests.

OutputA signed mechanic justification table, and the exact edit at which a mechanic flips.
Week three

The dark side, and the boundary

The same mechanics turned: loot boxes, gacha, streaks, engineered scarcity, and the bias set they run on. Then the clinical boundary, emotional architecture, and the question of what the design does by week nine.

OutputA boundary statement, a week-nine projection, and a fixed brief.
Week four

Build and measure

One playable or readable artifact for a named goal, handed to someone cold, measured on consenting peers, then given back to its players to say what they thought they agreed to.

OutputThe artifact, paired readings, and the players' own account of the pact.
02

Run sheets

Forty minutes of seminar, then the room works. Teams of three or four. Paper prototypes are acceptable throughout and no engine is prescribed — one of the three teaching cases is a board game, which settles that argument early.

Week one — The synergy and the pact

1.1

Why do you remember the story and not the statistic?

Beat
Narration as a force as ancient as the world: cave paintings, Sumerian law tablets, Greek myths — all of them narratives designed to educate. Transformative narration speaks to the emotional sphere, bypassing reason and promoting empathy. Zak's work on the dramatic arc, oxytocin, and mirror neurons as a virtual-reality experience that lets us step into another's shoes.
Evidence
Brockington and colleagues, on storytelling with eighty-one hospitalised children: oxytocin up 815 per cent, cortisol down 59 per cent, pain perception down 70 per cent, and more positive language than the riddle group. More than Words adds that a story told beats a story read aloud — greater vocabulary gain, better comprehension, more questions asked — and that live oral narration yields the best results of any condition.
Move
The Transport Test. Run it early so the recall check lands before the session ends.
Output
The room's own recall figure, on the wall, kept until session 3.1.
1.2

What is the difference between a game, game-based learning and gamification?

Beat
The distinction most of the field muddles, stated cleanly. A game is active storytelling: the player becomes the protagonist of a story that develops through their choices. Game-based learning uses an actual game as the medium of instruction. Gamification layers game elements — points, badges, leaderboards, progress bars — onto something that is not a game, to motivate behaviour. Gamification is not gaming, and conflating them is how both arguments about it go wrong.
Beat
Then the origin story. Malone at MIT showed the learning potential in the 1980s; the word spread in 2010 after Jesse Schell's D.I.C.E. talk, which described a world of connected soda cans and cereal boxes awarding points for brushing your teeth, driven by advertising and government incentive. The term became popular because the example was scandalous. That is worth sitting with: the field's popular birth was a critique mistaken for a roadmap.
Move
Teams sort twenty real products into the three categories. The disagreements are the lesson; the hard cases are usually the honest ones.
Output
A sorted wall, and a shared vocabulary for the rest of the course.
1.3

What is a pact, and when did you enter one?

Beat
Suspension of disbelief, described by Coleridge in 1817: engaging deeply with a fictional world without questioning its logic, accepting for a while the existence of fairies, elves, or cursed ghosts. The condition is that you knew you were entering it. Then what generative media broke — when a convincing world can be produced in seconds at negligible cost, the audience loses the one thing the pact depended on, which is the ability to tell which fictions it agreed to. Suspicion stops being a response to a claim and becomes the default posture toward everything, the honest kind included.
Move
Whose Fiction Is It.
Output
A sorted wall — pacts entered, pacts assumed, and a contested pile that becomes the syllabus for session 2.3.
1.4

What pact is this work asking for?

Beat
None. Analysis session.
Move
Teams produce a pact analysis of one existing narrative or game work: what it asks the audience to accept, whether it discloses the ask, where a first-time audience would actually meet that disclosure, and what the work then does with what it was given.
Output
The pact analysis. Assessed.

Week two — The permitted mechanics

2.1

What makes a system encourage growth from within?

Beat
Self-determination theory, in its operational form. Autonomy is player agency: the freedom and control a learner has over their decisions, actions and path, and the meaningful choices that make them invested. Competence is skill progression: gradually increasing challenge with improvement made visible, paced to keep the learner in the zone — challenged but not frustrated. Relatedness is social interaction: working with peers, which nurtures emotional intelligence and belonging, and through which students often learn better under adequate confrontation.
Beat
Then the point that saves the theory from being decoration. Games redefine failure: mistakes become opportunities to practise, fear of failure decreases, perseverance follows. Gamified training has been reported to raise motivation by around 44 per cent and to get tasks completed roughly 57 per cent faster — but those are engagement figures, and the course's whole argument is that engagement figures are not the question.
Move
Teams map a product they use against all three needs and mark which are genuinely met and which are simulated. A badge is not a competence; a follower count is not relatedness.
Output
Three columns per product, with the simulated ones named.
2.2

What is an archetype actually for?

Beat
Achievers, Explorers, Socializers, Killers — the player types, with the real-world parallels and the MBTI resemblances. Then the critical turn, which is the source thesis's own: these categories were used not only for design but for marketing audience segmentation and product tailoring. They help creators map experiences, reach wider audiences and make bigger sales, and they are absolutely not meant to define identities. When archetypes harden into labels they foster misunderstanding, exclusion, and subtle forms of discrimination. A person may be an Achiever at work, an Explorer at home, a Socializer among friends and a Killer in debate.
Beat
Then where that goes when the social scaffolding is missing: main character syndrome, a distorted pursuit of self-validation amplified by social media, which undermines relatedness and empathy and produces social disconnection and fragile self-worth. And its counterweight: reframing, the technique at the centre of cognitive behavioural therapy — shift the frame and the meaning of the situation changes, with thinking and behaviour following.
Move
Each team takes an archetype and designs the same feature twice: once treating the archetype as a state the person is in today, once as a label that defines them. Present both.
Output
Two versions of one feature, and a statement of what the second one costs.
2.3

Who holds the goal?

Beat
The three tests, stated plainly and used for the rest of the course. Who holds the goal. Are the mechanics disclosable. Does leaving cost nothing. Give the third one its provenance — the boat that became a safe place one couldn't easily leave — because a test with a story behind it is remembered and a test asserted from the front of the room is not.
Move
The Mechanic Trial, two rounds, using the contested pile from 1.3. The same mechanic is tried twice in different contexts and should be acquitted once.
Output
Two verdicts on one mechanic, which is the point.
2.4

Where exactly does it flip?

Beat
Brief: the boundary between support and capture is usually one design decision wide, and a practitioner who cannot locate it will cross it by accident.
Move
Goal Theft. Then teams complete the mechanic justification table for what they intend to build — every mechanic, who holds the goal, how it is disclosed, what leaving costs, and the ruling.
Output
The signed justification table. Mechanics not on it may not appear in the build.

Week three — The dark side, and the boundary

3.1

What happens when these mechanics are turned?

Beat
The same communicative wonders that inspired learning and fostered empathy, used to deceive, addict and control. Likes, shares and streaks triggering dopamine release and compelling return. Loot boxes and gacha mechanics that mimic gambling, linked in the literature to gambling addiction among younger players — banned outright in Belgium and the Netherlands, age-restricted in Japan. The Labubu case: fairy-tale storytelling plus gacha, around four hundred million dollars in a year inside a one-point-eight-billion-dollar total, built on a character whose story is a small tragedy, which is exactly reliable for an audience seeking emotional comfort and escapism.
Beat
And the bias set the whole apparatus runs on: confirmation bias, illusory superiority and its counterpart the illusion of inferiority, locus of control, loss aversion — marketing's favourite — fear of missing out, illusory correlation, information bias, clustering illusion. Then the figures that make it a public matter rather than a design preference: the rise in adolescent depression and anxiety from the early 2010s, with self-harm up sharply among teenage girls and far more sharply among preteens.
Move
Each team takes one mechanic from its own justification table and builds the extractive version of it. Twenty minutes. Then runs the three tests on what it just built.
Output
A paired honest-and-extractive version of one mechanic, with both rulings written down.
3.2

What can design not do here?

Beat
The boundary, taught as method rather than attached as a disclaimer. The source thesis is exact about it: the symbolic architecture, the sensory rhythm and the emotional scaffolding of Coil Hibiki were not to make a diagnosis — they were resonance, understanding and connection. Design can shape an environment, carry a practice someone has already been given, lower the cost of a good habit and make a difficult thing approachable. It cannot assess, diagnose or hold risk. Where it would need to, it needs a clinician, and the design's job is to make that handover easy rather than to substitute for it.
Beat
Then emotional architecture, which is what design can do: catharsis, emotional release in metaphorical and controlled environments; reflection, revisiting choices without real-world stakes; regulation, practising fear, empathy and guilt in low-pressure scenarios. And the thing most teams cut first — quiet moments, which mirror mindfulness practice, help regulate anxiety between intense scenes, and are never filler.
Move
Each team writes its boundary statement: what the artifact does, what it explicitly does not claim, what happens when a user is beyond its scope, and where in the artifact a person will actually meet this.
Output
The boundary statement. It goes into the artifact, not the documentation.
3.3

Could they leave?

Beat
Flow, in Csikszentmihalyi's terms and in the four conditions the source thesis states: clear goals and instant feedback; a gradual increase in difficulty; environments that remove distractions; rewards that reinforce mastery. Then the trap, which is that all four can be satisfied by something a person cannot put down. Flow is the course's most powerful tool and its most dangerous one, and the test for it is the third test.
Move
The Boat Problem.
Output
A week-nine projection and, for most teams, a design change. Assessed.
3.4

What are you building, and for whom?

Beat
None. Briefing session.
Move
Briefs fixed. Each team names the learner or the goal specifically, states which mechanics it is forbidding itself, and confirms the consent protocol for week four. Any proposal involving minors or patients is redirected here, while the brief is still movable — not in week four.
Output
A fixed brief and a signed constraint sheet.

Week four — Build and measure

4.1

Does it work at all?

Beat
None. Build, supervised. Open the three cases on the wall as precedents, not as models to copy.
Move
Facilitator circulates with one question: which mechanic did you just reach for, and which test did you run on it?
Output
A playable draft by the end of the session. Playable, not finished.
4.2

Does it work for someone who is not you?

Beat
One rule, from the source thesis's account of designing for VR: a good designer must always assume the user is totally clueless. In an unfamiliar medium that is not unflattering, it is foundational — a first-time user, unprompted, will stand still and look around for a surprisingly long time.
Move
Teams swap artifacts and play each other's cold, with no explanation offered. The builder may not speak. Everything the player has to ask is a finding.
Output
The list of questions players had to ask, and one revision pass.
4.3

What state does it leave them in?

Beat
The paired reading: the restoration instrument shared with the rest of the programme, and an SDT reading built from the three needs as the source thesis defines them. Tell the room what each one is before either is used.
Move
Readings taken on consenting peers under the protocol. Written down before anyone discusses them.
Output
Paired readings per subject.
4.4

Would they say they agreed to it?

Beat
The facilitator does not speak for the first hour.
Move
The Pact Reading.
Output
The players' own account of what they agreed to, set beside what the team built. Assessed.
03

Six moves

Five games and a closing session that deliberately is not one. A course about play has an obvious temptation here, so it is worth naming: nothing in this kit awards points that persist, keeps a leaderboard or records a streak. A course teaching the three tests cannot run on mechanics that fail them.

Session 1.1 · 45 minutes, split · whole cohort

The Transport Test

The room is taught the same content twice, in two halves, one as information and one as narrative. Recall is checked twenty minutes later, before anyone knows a check is coming.

  1. Split the room. Each half gets one version, out of earshot of the other.
  2. Teach something genuinely dull and factual — a procedure, a set of figures, a sequence of dates.
  3. Tell the narrative version aloud rather than reading it. The evidence is specifically that telling beats reading, and that gesture, vocal variation and eye contact carry much of the effect.
  4. Carry on for twenty minutes, then run an unannounced written free recall. Count items retrieved per person and put both figures on the wall.

The narrative half usually wins, and occasionally it does not, which is better. Either way the room now has its own number instead of a claim from a reading, and it gets compared with the scaffold they build in week three. Step three is the part facilitators skip and should not.

Session 1.3 · 50 minutes · whole cohort

Whose Fiction Is It

Students bring a piece of media and the room rules on one thing: was a pact entered, or assumed?

  1. Describe the work and what it asks the audience to accept.
  2. The room votes: entered, assumed, or contested.
  3. For anything ruled assumed, name the moment at which the audience should have been told and was not.
  4. Keep the contested pile. It is the syllabus for session 2.3.

Students arrive able to tell advertising from art and unable to say why, which leaves them defenceless when the two are blended — which is what transmedia does by design. Making the ruling the unit of work rather than the genre establishes that the question is disclosure.

Session 2.3 · 80 minutes · two teams plus a bench

The Mechanic Trial

A mechanic is tried in a named context. Prosecution argues extraction, defence argues pact, and the bench rules on the three tests only.

  1. Draw a mechanic and a context. Fifteen minutes to prepare each side.
  2. Prosecution, then defence. Six minutes each, then questions from the bench.
  3. The bench rules test by test — goal, disclosure, exit — and may not rule on whether it likes the mechanic.
  4. Retry the same mechanic in a different context with the sides swapped.

A mechanic acquitted in one context and convicted in another, by the same bench, twenty minutes apart, is the course's thesis demonstrated rather than argued. Draw the contexts so this happens at least once in the first round.

Session 2.4 · 55 minutes · teams of three

Goal Theft

Start with a mechanic that genuinely serves the person. Change it as little as possible until it serves the system instead. Find the exact edit.

  1. Describe the honest version precisely enough to build.
  2. Make the smallest single change that moves the goal from the person to the system.
  3. Present both versions and the one edit between them.
  4. The room challenges: was that the smallest possible change? Usually it was not, and a smaller one is worse.

The boundary turns out to be one decision wide — a default, a visibility, a reset condition, a timing — and having located it once by hand, a designer stops crossing it by accident. The real precedent is in the source thesis: Coil Hibiki's progress rewards are offered as journal accessories and playlists the player is not forced to take if they do not need to. Remove that clause and the same reward becomes an obligation.

Session 3.3 · 70 minutes · teams

The Boat Problem

The flow state is the thing this course is best at producing and the thing most likely to go wrong. Teams find in their own design what the author of the source thesis found in hers.

  1. Read the Cloudy Horizons passage aloud: a flying boat, a minimal interface with no pressuring health or hunger statistics and no notifications, repetitive calming loops — and then the designer's own observation that the flow was capturing, and the boat could become a safe place one couldn't easily leave.
  2. Each team checks its own design against the four flow conditions, and confirms it satisfies them.
  3. Then the real question: what in your design makes leaving cost something? Count it in steps, in lost progress, and in how the design makes going feel.
  4. Describe the person in week one and the same person in week nine, assuming nothing improved and nothing was added. If what they can no longer do without it is something they could previously do unaided, the design has a finding against it and changes.

This is the session students report back on. Wellbeing and education products are almost always evaluated at the point of maximum novelty, which is when they look best and tell you least — and a design good enough to produce flow is a design good enough to trap someone in it. Running the exercise on a real case where a real designer caught herself is what stops the room treating it as a hypothetical.

Session 4.4 · 90 minutes · whole cohort

The Pact Reading

Not a game. The people who played each artifact say, in their own words and with the builders silent, what they understood themselves to have agreed to.

  1. Players speak first. Builders may not introduce, frame or correct.
  2. The team then reads out what it intended the pact to be.
  3. Where the two differ, the team says which one is true of the thing they built.
  4. Readings are stated with the same plainness whether or not they flatter. The room may ask, and may not congratulate.

Consent is the course's whole criterion and it is not something a designer can self-assess. If the players' account does not match the intention, the intention was not communicated and the design does not have the consent it claims, whatever the readings say.

05

The three cases

Taught as precedents, not as models. Each one is a real project with real constraints, and each carries one lesson the course cannot teach any other way. Put all three on the wall in week four.

VALLAE — game-based learning in mixed reality

An educational escape room built in XR around a virtual reconstruction of the Renaissance philologist Lorenzo Valla's study, teaching Roman sites, figures and philological concepts. What makes it a teaching case is the constraint work. Difficulty is held constant rather than exponential, to keep an even rhythm and avoid both tedium and manufactured urgency. The time restriction — normally an enemy — is used deliberately, both to bound how much a person has to learn in one sitting and to limit the disorientation and headache that prolonged VR produces. Movement in VR is limited to teleportation while players move freely in the real room.

And the mechanic worth stealing: players in VR and players in reality alternate on every challenge and must guide each other across the two. Cooperation is what turns the cognitive biases into a bonus — confirmation bias and illusory superiority become visible to the people experiencing them, and get resolved through dialogue rather than instruction.

Teach it for: designing an educational constraint on purpose; the clue that only works because it is withheld; bias made useful by being made social.

Cloudy Horizons — flow, and its ethical problem

A flying boat above a lost Earth: check the systems, tend the plants, keep the birds safe, make tea, read letters that arrive by bird, organise your thoughts in a three-dimensional realm that opens from a desk. Anxiety-informed design, with escalating tension met through manageable exposure so a player learns to tolerate it without panic — courage practised through a story rather than a speech. The interface is deliberately minimal: no pressuring health or hunger statistics, no overwhelming notifications. Ambient sound does the work — engines, birds, creaking fire, bubbling from the kitchen as a gentle signal that cooking is nearly ready.

It satisfies all four flow conditions, and the designer then noticed the problem herself: the flow was capturing, and the boat could become a safe place one couldn't easily leave. That observation is the third test, and this case is where the room meets it.

Teach it for: flow built deliberately; the restorative interface as a set of removals; and a practitioner catching her own design in the act.

Coil Hibiki — self-determination made analogue

A board game and a journal, deliberately on paper, with a website behind a unique code. Built against the blank first page of a journal nobody ever writes in. The three needs are mapped explicitly: autonomy, because the player shapes the journey rather than following it; competence, because progress is felt rather than scored and the small rewards — journal accessories, new playlists — are offered and not imposed; relatedness, through echoes of lost souls and memories carried by symbolic immersion rather than dialogue trees.

The mechanics are chosen against research into cognitive decline: alternating typefaces as mnemonic cues that switch the reader's inner tone of voice; colour-coded symbol slots where the player picks which prompt suits today, with the meditative lake pages distinguished from the shorter leaf ones; sudoku and crosswords as locks to break, training recall and logical flow; and colouring, which is naturally gamified because the motivation is simply to see the finished image. The letters are cryptic on purpose, to make the mind remember, connect and reframe. The AI layer translates in-game actions into personal insight, and the stated ambition is to train a model with professional assistance — not to diagnose.

Teach it for: a reward the player may decline; a prompt the player chooses; and a wellbeing design that states what it is not.

Assessment

No exam. One artifact, and the account of why every mechanic in it is there.

PieceWeightGraded on
The artifact35%Whether it holds the signed constraint sheet, whether a cold player can use it unaided, and whether the boundary statement is in the thing rather than the documentation.
The mechanic justification30%Every mechanic ruled on all three tests, rejections included. The rejections carry as much of the mark as the inclusions.
The pact reading20%The gap between what players said they agreed to and what the team intended, and the honesty of the team's account of it.
The boat problem15%Whether the cost of leaving was found before the build and designed out, rather than noticed afterwards.

Two rules stated in session 1.1. A mechanic correctly rejected is worth as much as one well used, and a team whose justification table contains no rejections will be asked what it considered. And the paired readings are graded on protocol, never on the numbers — a measured depletion, honestly obtained and correctly interpreted, scores above an unmeasured claim of restoration.

07

Teaching notes

State the contradiction in the first ten minutes. Students who have taken the first course know it forbids streaks and leaderboards outright. Read the §2.1 passage, give the three tests, give the third one its provenance, and move on. If you do not, half the room spends two weeks waiting for you to notice.

Teach the three-way distinction before anything else. Game, game-based learning, gamification. Almost every bad argument in this field — in both directions — comes from collapsing the three, and the room cannot rule on a mechanic it cannot classify.

Gamification is not the vocabulary. The word carries a decade of bad practice and students reach for its techniques as a kit. If a team says they are gamifying something, ask who holds the goal and the conversation recovers.

Draw trial contexts so a mechanic gets acquitted. If the first round convicts everything, the room concludes the tests are decoration and that this is really the first course again. Make sure one well-argued acquittal happens early.

Tell the Transport Test story rather than reading it. The evidence is specifically that telling beats reading aloud, and that gesture, vocal variation and eye contact carry much of the effect. A facilitator who reads the narrative version from a script has run the wrong experiment and will get a flat result.

Hold the clinical boundary without making the course timid. Say plainly in 3.2 that nothing here trains anyone to treat anyone, then let students design ambitiously inside that limit. The boundary constrains claims, not ambition — the Coil Hibiki case is the proof, since it researched depression, anxiety and dementia, consulted people who work with patients, and still stated that it was not there to diagnose.

Redirect protected-population proposals in 3.4, not in week four. Students will want to test with children, patients or their own support groups. They design for that population and test with consenting adult peers unless institutional ethics review is already in hand, and the time to say so is while the brief can still move.

Say what the self-determination reading is. It is assembled for the course from the three needs, it is grounded, and it is not validated. A course whose criterion is disclosed consent cannot hand students an instrument with an unstated provenance.
