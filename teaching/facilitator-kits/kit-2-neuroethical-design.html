Facilitator's kit · course two

Human-Centric and Neuroethical Design

Four weeks on what a design decision does to a nervous system, and what follows from that. Students do not build an artifact in this course. They produce the neuroethical design review a company would have to answer, and then defend it against a room playing the team that shipped the thing.

4 weeks · 16 sessions48 contact hoursSeminar and review
01

The map

This is the portable course. It needs no equipment, no studio brief and no commercial framing, and it runs unchanged as a faculty elective or as professional training. The pedagogy is adversarial rather than generative: students are not asked to make something good, they are asked to judge something real and hold the judgement under pressure.

Week one

Design is a neurological act

Every colour, typeface, spatial and motion decision activating or suppressing cognitive systems. Neuroplasticity and scale. Why the ignorance defence fails once the mechanism is named.

OutputA named mechanism register for one product, and a failed ignorance defence.
Week two

The person, not the profile

What behavioural data captures and what it cannot. The three postures as working practice. Vulnerability as a design variable rather than an edge case.

OutputA vulnerability analysis: the state in which the design becomes harmful, and who is routinely in it.
Week three

Neuroethics in practice

Consent where there is no consent form. Manipulation as influence that does not sufficiently engage reflection. What is now actionable rather than merely objectionable.

OutputThe consent dialogue the interface would need, and a regulatory exposure note.
Week four

The review

Assembling the findings into a document with a verdict and required remediations, then defending it against the people who would have to act on it.

OutputThe review document, the hearing, and a public record of what was amended.
02

Run sheets

Forty minutes of seminar, then the room works. Teams of four, each team assigned one shipping product in week one and keeping it for the whole four weeks.

Week one — Design is a neurological act

1.1

Why did you leave that room?

Beat
You have walked into a room and felt, before a single conscious thought formed, that something was wrong. That was a nervous system reading an environment and reporting before the prefrontal cortex had a chance to process it. Design, spatial or interactive, is never neutral.
Move
The Room You Left. Products assigned at the end of the session; teams keep them to week four.
Output
A list of mechanisms the room identified without anyone having a vocabulary yet.
1.2

What is actually being activated?

Beat
The named mechanisms and their cognitive targets. Notification badges, red dots, the removed stopping cue — neurological decisions made by designers who either understood the consequences or produced them by default. Both are failures.
Move
The Mechanism Deck, two rounds. Teams must name the target and the population most exposed, not just the mechanic.
Output
A shared vocabulary, and the first rows of the mechanism register.
1.3

What does this do at scale, over years?

Beat
The brain as a continuously restructuring system that rewires around repeated stimuli. An interface optimised for arousal and deployed daily to millions is not extracting attention in the moment; it is restructuring the attentional architecture of a population. At that timescale the anxiety passes from side effect to physical alteration.
Move
Teams complete the mechanism register for their assigned product: every mechanism, its target, the exposed population, and the daily frequency of exposure.
Output
The mechanism register. Assessed as part of the review.
1.4

Can you still say you did not know?

Beat
Someone who understands the mechanism and deploys high-arousal design anyway is making a public health decision on behalf of an unconsenting population. The question the session settles is what happens to the defence of not having known.
Move
The Ignorance Defence.
Output
Nothing written. The room has watched a defence stop working, which is the premise the next three weeks stand on.

Week two — The person, not the profile

2.1

What does the data actually know?

Beat
A dataset records what someone did under specific conditions at a specific moment. It says nothing about the state they were in, are in, or will be in when the next thing arrives. Most behaviour is processed below conscious awareness by systems the person cannot report on, so behavioural data captures the output of processes the person has no access to.
Beat
Where this argument comes from is worth saying, because students assume it is an ethics position and it is not — it is an accuracy position that happens to have ethical consequences. It appears in game design before it appears in marketing: the player archetypes used to map motivation were drawn up partly for audience segmentation and product tailoring, and the critique of them is that they were never meant to define identities. When archetypes harden into labels they foster misunderstanding, exclusion and subtle forms of discrimination. A person may be an Achiever at work, an Explorer at home, a Socializer among friends and a Killer in debate. The same objection, in the same form, is what this course makes of a targeting model.
Move
Teams take their product's own stated targeting model and write the three people it describes equally well who would need opposite treatment.
Output
Three counter-cases per model.
2.2

What can this person receive right now?

Beat
The three cognitive postures, taught once and used thereafter. Directed attention as a finite resource; what happens past the ceiling. Soft fascination as the opposite condition.
Move
Teams map which posture their product is designed for, which it actually receives most often, and what the gap costs the user.
Output
A posture mismatch finding.
2.3

Who breaks first?

Beat
Vulnerability as a design variable, not an accessibility afterthought: age, disability, economic circumstance, and the ordinary depletion that puts anybody into a vulnerable state by eleven at night. A design is not safe because most users tolerate it.
Move
The Vulnerable Case.
Output
The vulnerability analysis. Assessed.
2.4

Where is the line between influence and manipulation?

Beat
Manipulation defined as influence that does not sufficiently engage a person's capacity for reflection and deliberation, and the publicity principle behind it: a practice is suspect when those who use it would not defend it openly to the people it is used on.
Move
Each team argues that one mechanism in their own product falls on the legitimate side. The room tests it against both criteria. Most arguments fail; the ones that survive are the interesting result.
Output
A defensible list and an indefensible list.

Week three — Neuroethics in practice

3.1

What would consent even look like here?

Beat
There is no consent form in an interface. There is a design that either engages the person's reflective will or routes around it. The question is what disclosure would have to say for the mechanics to be genuinely agreed to.
Move
Consent Without a Form.
Output
The consent dialogue. Assessed.
3.2

What is already actionable?

Beat
The regulatory ground as it now stands: prohibitions on techniques that work outside conscious awareness or exploit vulnerabilities tied to age, disability or circumstance; duties attached to algorithmic recommendation, including provision to switch it off; protections for minors; and data protection regimes that bear on profiling. The point is the shift from wrong to actionable.
Move
Teams produce a regulatory exposure note using the triage instrument: for each mechanism, which regime a lawyer would be asked about, and what the team would need to know to answer.
Output
The exposure note, written as questions for counsel rather than as conclusions.
3.3

What would you require them to change?

Beat
A finding without a remediation is a complaint. A remediation that would destroy the product is not a remediation. The craft of this session is proposing the smallest change that removes the harm.
Move
For each indefensible mechanism, teams write the minimum viable remediation and estimate what it costs the business. Where the honest answer is that it costs the business model, they say so.
Output
A remediation schedule.
3.4

Does the verdict hold together?

Beat
None. Drafting session.
Move
Teams assemble the review document and run it through the five-question gate. Cross-reading: every team reads another team's draft and marks every finding that could not be defended by someone who disagreed with it.
Output
The review document, circulated to the team that will oppose it.

Week four — The review

4.1

What is the strongest case against you?

Beat
Teams are told who will oppose them and given the session to prepare that opposition's best argument rather than their own.
Move
Each team writes the three strongest objections to its own review and answers them in writing. Unanswered objections must be conceded in the document before the hearing, not during it.
Output
A pre-conceded list. Conceding here costs nothing; conceding under questioning costs the finding.
4.2

Can you hold it?

Beat
None.
Move
The Hearing, first half of the cohort.
Output
The defence, and findings marked held or amended.
4.3

Can you hold it?

Beat
None.
Move
The Hearing, second half.
Output
As above.
4.4

What are you amending?

Beat
The facilitator does not speak for the first hour.
Move
Amend or Hold.
Output
The final review, with the amendment record attached. Assessed.
03

Six moves

This course is argued rather than built, so its moves are adversarial. Nothing here awards points that persist, keeps a leaderboard or records a streak.

Session 1.1 · 40 minutes · whole cohort

The Room You Left

Each student describes a place or an interface they left sooner than they intended, without being able to say why. No analysis from the speaker.

  1. Describe only what was there. Colour, sound, density, movement, what was being asked of you.
  2. The room proposes what the nervous system was reading.
  3. The speaker says whether it rings true. They are not obliged to agree.
  4. Keep a wall list of every mechanism proposed, in the room's own untrained words.

The list is referred back to in week three, by which point the room has the vocabulary. Watching their own untrained descriptions turn out to have been accurate is what establishes that this is readable, not speculative.

Session 1.4 · 55 minutes · one volunteer, then all

The Ignorance Defence

A mock hearing in two passes. The same defence is offered twice and works only once.

  1. A volunteer defends a real design decision on the ground that they did not know what it did. The room must accept it.
  2. The facilitator then names the mechanism precisely, with the research behind it.
  3. The volunteer offers the same defence again. The room now rules on it.
  4. Every student writes one decision in their own past work that this just disqualified.

The course's central claim is that the ignorance defence does not survive the mechanism being named, and that both deploying it knowingly and producing it by default are failures. Asserting that produces nodding. Running it twenty minutes apart produces the uncomfortable private list in step four, which is the actual learning.

Session 2.3 · 60 minutes · teams of four

The Vulnerable Case

Teams work their own assigned product and find the state in which it becomes harmful rather than merely annoying.

  1. Name the state precisely. Not a vulnerable user but the specific condition — depleted, grieving, in debt, fourteen, in pain, newly diagnosed.
  2. Name the population routinely in that state, and estimate what share of the user base it is.
  3. Describe the harm concretely enough that someone could check whether it happens.
  4. The room challenges the estimate. A team that cannot support a number must widen or withdraw it.

Vulnerability is normally taught as a category of person, which lets a designer treat it as someone else's problem. Teaching it as a state everybody enters forces the finding into the main design rather than the accessibility appendix.

Session 3.1 · 65 minutes · teams, then whole cohort

Consent Without a Form

Write the disclosure the interface would have to show for its actual mechanics to be genuinely agreed to. Then read it out.

  1. Plain language, second person, no euphemism. Name the mechanism and what it is designed to produce in the reader.
  2. No more than 120 words. A disclosure nobody would read is not a disclosure.
  3. Read it aloud as though it appeared on first open.
  4. The room answers one question: would the company ship this screen?

Almost every dialogue the room writes is unshippable, and the reason is not that it is badly written. It is the same hard gate as the five-question standard arriving from a different direction: a mechanic that cannot survive its own disclosure was never disclosable. Students find this out by writing it rather than by being told.

Sessions 4.2–4.3 · 3 hours each · pairs of teams

The Hearing

Each team presents its review to another team, which plays the product team that shipped the thing and defends it in good faith.

  1. Twenty minutes to present the findings, the verdict and the remediations.
  2. Twenty-five minutes of opposition. The defending team argues the real business case, not a caricature.
  3. The reviewing team must respond to every objection on the spot: hold, amend, or concede.
  4. Roles swap. Everyone does both.

The defending role is the one that teaches. Having to argue sincerely for a design you spent three weeks condemning is the only reliable way to find out whether your findings were sound or merely righteous.

Session 4.4 · 90 minutes · whole cohort

Amend or Hold

Not a game. Each team states aloud which findings it is amending after the hearing and which it holds, and why.

  1. Amendments are read first, before anything held.
  2. No defending an amendment. State it and move on.
  3. A finding held must be held with the objection repeated accurately alongside it.
  4. The room may ask. The room may not congratulate.

An honest amendment scores above a finding held through stubbornness, and above a finding that was never tested. This is where the course's own standard — would you be comfortable if they could see exactly what you are doing and why — gets applied to the reviewer instead of to the product.

Instruments

Three instruments for use in the room, plus the gate shared with the rest of the programme. They keep nothing: no entry is stored, transmitted or counted.

Instrument one

The Mechanism Deck

Draw a mechanism. Teams name the cognitive target and the population most exposed before anything is revealed. Naming the mechanic alone does not score.

Instrument two

The vulnerability matrix

Pick a state and a population. The instrument returns the design obligations that follow and the questions the review must answer. It is a prompt, not an answer: the obligations are generic and the team's job is to make each one specific to the product in front of them.

—

    Instrument three

    Regulatory exposure triage

    Select what a mechanism does. The instrument returns which regimes a review would have to ask about and what the team needs to establish before anyone can answer. It produces questions, never conclusions.

    Select what the mechanism does.

      What this is not This is a teaching aid for structuring a review, not legal advice, and it is deliberately built to output questions rather than findings. Jurisdictions differ, the instruments it names are summarised rather than quoted, and whether any provision applies to a given product is a question for someone qualified to answer it. A student review states its regulatory section as questions for counsel, and a review that states a legal conclusion is marked down for doing so.

      Instrument four

      The five-question gate

      Shared with the rest of the programme, and run here on the review rather than on a design. Questions three and four are hard gates. Applied to a reviewer, question four reads: would you be comfortable if the team you are judging could see exactly how you reached this?

      Answer the five to get a verdict.

      05

      Assessment

      One document, built over four weeks and defended once. No exam.

      PieceWeightGraded on
      The review document40%Whether each finding is specific, supported and remediable, and whether the verdict follows from the findings rather than preceding them.
      The hearing25%Both roles. Holding a sound finding under pressure, and arguing the opposing case in good faith rather than as a straw man.
      The amendment record20%Honest revision. An amendment made cleanly scores above a weak finding defended to the end, and above one never tested.
      The consent dialogue15%Whether it names the mechanism without euphemism and stays inside 120 words.

      Two rules stated in session 1.1. A regulatory section written as questions for counsel scores above one written as conclusions, every time. And a review that finds a product largely defensible, with the evidence to support that, is worth exactly as much as one that condemns it — a cohort that only ever convicts has learned a posture rather than a method.

      06

      Teaching notes

      Assign products you would defend. If every assigned product is an obvious villain, the hearing has no tension and the course teaches contempt. Include at least two products the room broadly likes, and one the facilitator uses daily.

      Play the defending team yourself, once. In the first hearing, take the product team's side and argue it properly. The room needs to see that the opposing case can be strong before it will take the role seriously in the second session.

      Do not let the regulatory week become a law seminar. The content is the shift from objectionable to actionable, not the provisions themselves. If a student wants the provisions, send them to someone qualified and move on.

      Refuse the moral register. The course argues from mechanism and consequence, not from character. A student calling a designer evil has stopped doing the work; ask them which mechanism, which state, which population, and the argument either recovers or dissolves.

      This course has no artifact, and students will ask for one. Hold the line. The document is the deliverable, and the reason is that a practitioner who can only make and cannot judge is the person this course exists to stop producing.

      Run the gate on yourself at the end. Answer question four out loud about your own teaching of the course, in front of the cohort, and say what you would change. It is the same demand the course makes of everyone in the room.
